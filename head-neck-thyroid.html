Head and Neck (Ear-Nose & Throat) Cancer

Multidisciplinary Treatment Approach

Early Detection. Comprehensive Care. Better Outcomes.

Common Head and Neck Cancers
  • Oral cavity cancer
  • Oropharyngeal cancer (HPV-related and non-HPV)
  • Laryngeal cancer
  • Hypopharyngeal cancer
  • Nasal cavity and paranasal sinus cancer
  • Salivary gland tumors
  • Thyroid cancer (see right panel)
  • Skin cancers of the head and neck
Common Symptoms
  • Persistent sore throat
  • Difficulty swallowing
  • Hoarseness or voice changes
  • Persistent mouth ulcers
  • Neck mass or swelling
  • Ear pain
  • Unexplained weight loss
  • Persistent nasal obstruction or bleeding

Standard Treatment for Locally Advanced Head and Neck Cancer

Concurrent Chemoradiation (Standard of Care)

1
Chemotherapy

Cisplatin 100 mg/m² every 3 weeks (Days 1, 22, 43) OR Cisplatin 40 mg/m² weekly (6–7 weeks)

Alternatives (when cisplatin not suitable): Carboplatin + 5-Fluorouracil; Cetuximab (selected patients)

2
Radiotherapy

External beam radiotherapy to the primary tumor and regional lymph nodes

Typical total dose: 70 Gy in 35 fractions (2 Gy per fraction over 7 weeks)

Intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) when available

Daily image guidance (IGRT)

3
Supportive Care

Nutritional support (oral or feeding tube)

Speech and swallowing rehabilitation

Pain management

Management of side effects (mucositis, dermatitis, dry mouth, etc.)

Psychosocial support

Regular follow-up

Thyroid Cancer

Evidence-Based Treatment Including I-131

Surgery First. Radioiodine (I-131) When Indicated. Radiotherapy When Other Options Fail.

Types of Thyroid Cancer
  • Papillary thyroid cancer (most common)
  • Follicular thyroid cancer
  • Medullary thyroid cancer
  • Anaplastic thyroid cancer
  • Other rare types
Common Symptoms
  • Neck lump or nodule
  • Swelling in the neck
  • Hoarseness
  • Difficulty swallowing (rare)
  • Breathing changes (rare)

Treatment of Thyroid Cancer

Individualized Based on Type, Stage and Risk Factors

1
Surgery (First-line Treatment)

Total or partial thyroidectomy depending on the type and stage

Removal of involved lymph nodes when indicated

2
Radioiodine Therapy with I-131

Iodine-131 (I-131) is a radioactive isotope selectively taken up by thyroid tissue

Used after surgery to destroy any remaining thyroid cancer cells (especially in differentiated thyroid cancers)

Helps reduce the risk of recurrence

Administered as an oral capsule in a controlled setting

Well tolerated with minimal side effects

3
Thyroid Hormone Suppression

Lifelong levothyroxine to keep TSH low and reduce recurrence risk

Regular monitoring of thyroid hormone levels (TSH, free T4)

4
Radiotherapy (When Other Options Fail)

Radiotherapy is generally NOT a routine treatment for thyroid cancer

Considered when surgery and radioiodine (I-131) are not possible or have failed

Locally advanced, unresectable disease

Persistent or recurrent disease not responsive to radioiodine

Palliative treatment for symptom control (e.g. bone metastases)

5
Follow-up

Periodic clinical evaluation, neck ultrasound and lab tests (thyroglobulin, TSH)

Additional imaging (CT, MRI, or SPECT-CT) when indicated

Long-term surveillance for recurrence

Key Message

  • Most thyroid cancers are successfully treated with surgery and radioiodine (I-131)
  • Radiotherapy is reserved for selected cases when other options fail
  • Early detection and appropriate treatment lead to excellent outcomes in most patients
